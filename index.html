I work in philosophical logic and the philosophy of logic and mathematics, and more occasionally in metaphysics and the philosophy of language.

Most of my current work is in metasemantics; in particular, how logical and mathematical vocabulary comes to mean what it does. I am mainly concerned with questions about categoricity and determinacy raised by limiting metatheoretic results such as Gödelian incompleteness, compactness, and the Löwenheim–Skolem theorems, and with how far views like logical inferentialism and mathematical conventionalism can get around them. I am also interested in logical and mathematical pluralism, higher-order logic, absolute generality, logicality, and quantifier variance. My earlier work was on truth, with a focus on deflationism, disquotation, and the patterns of reference behind the paradoxes.

I am an editor of Philosophical Logic and a subject editor of the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. I took my PhD at the University of Buenos Aires in 2015 and held positions at LMU Munich and UCL before moving to Singapore.

Research

For a word like “cat” there is a plausible story about how the meaning gets fixed: we point at cats while saying it. For “not” and “plus” there is no such story. Inferentialists hold that the meaning of logical and mathematical vocabulary is fixed exclusively by inference rules. But any rules we are in a position to follow can plausibly be captured by a recursively axiomatisable theory — and Carnap's categoricity problem, the Löwenheim–Skolem theorems and Gödel's first incompleteness theorem each show that theories of that kind fail to pin down the meanings we take ourselves to have. How far the inferentialist can withstand this is the question most of my work addresses.

Quantification

Mysterious Quantifiers argues that Carnap's problem for the first-order quantifiers resists the usual solutions, and that retreating to an infinitary calculus does not help either. Inferentialism then forces a revisionary view on which quantified sentences have what I call sentential truth-conditions. A New Semantics for Higher-Orderese develops that semantics into a way out of the impasse in higher-order metaphysics: it is given in a familiar first-order language, as the higher-order sceptic demands, while avoiding any reading of higher-order quantification as generalisation over a domain of objects.

Determinacy and pluralism

With Daniel Waxman: Internalism and the Determinacy of Mathematics argues that Button and Walsh's categoricity and intolerance results do not deliver determinacy, being schematic and leaving no fact of the matter as to which way a sentence goes. Arithmetical Pluralism and the Objectivity of Syntax dismantles the Parsons and Putnam–Koellner objections, replaces them with a stronger argument that pluralism about arithmetic entails pluralism about syntax, and finds that a stable pluralist position survives it.

Formality

Logical constants are usually called formal on the grounds that their semantic values are invariant under substitutions of individuals. Every invariance account faces well-known difficulties, and there is reason to think the difficulties come from the focus on semantic values. Formality Without Invariance works out an inferentialist alternative in which constants are interpreted by sets of rules and invariance drops out of the picture entirely.

In progress

Whether open-endedness rescues categoricity arguments — I argue that Brincus's objection would sink almost all of them if it worked, and that it does not. Whether we can, as Warren claims, follow the ω-rule. And whether mathematical conventionalism survives the observation that induction schemas admit instances containing empirical vocabulary.

The long-term aim is a metasemantic account of logic and mathematics, most likely along inferentialist lines. My own inclination is that on such an account logical and mathematical content turns out to be highly indeterminate — not through any defect in the account, but because that is how things stand.

Abstract

A well-known problem, due to Carnap, concerns how the meanings of logical connectives can possibly arise from the rules governing their use. Much less discussed is an analogous problem for quantifiers. This paper argues that the problem for quantifiers is serious, resists the usual solutions, and (on plausible semantic and metasemantic assumptions) forces us into a radically revisionary view of quantifiers and the truth-conditions of quantified sentences.
